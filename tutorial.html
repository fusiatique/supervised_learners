Explore the minimal dependence model

A hands-on guide: run the simulation, make an agent dependent, and find out what protects it.

Introduction

Goal. This simulation explores one possible explanation for drug dependence: how can an agent that simply learns from reward come to choose a drug again and again, even when every use costs it something? The agent is never told that the drug is good or bad. It only learns which actions keep it feeling right.

Purpose. The simulation lets you watch dependence develop step by step, and test what causes it and what protects against it: forced doses, the drug’s cost, abstinence and relapse, and how quickly the baseline heals. It is a teaching tool for a computational hypothesis, not a model of real brains or a clinical tool. All numbers are illustrative.

By the end of this tutorial you will have made an agent dependent, seen why it keeps using the drug, and found out what protects it. Section 1 explains how the model works.

How to use this tutorial

The framed panels with Play buttons are the real simulation, not pictures. Each one runs its own agent, so you can experiment freely without affecting the others. If a panel gets into a state you don’t want, press Restart panel under it to get its starting agent back.

Each section ends with a Try it box. Before opening What should happen?, make a guess: predicting first is the quickest way to find out whether the model works the way you think it does.

In the simulation’s panels the internal level H is labelled Current State (D). It is the same variable.

1. The question

How can repeated drug use make a drug increasingly valuable, even when every use carries a cost? This simulation explores one possible mechanism: drug use gradually lowers an internal baseline, until natural rewards can no longer keep the agent where it wants to be, and the drug becomes the only thing that can.

The agent cares about two internal variables:

  • Internal level H: how the agent is doing right now. It wants H at a target called the setpoint, 50, and is rewarded for moving H towards it.
  • Baseline B: the level H drifts back to. Each step, H moves 10% of the way towards B. B starts at 40. Each drug use lowers it, and it slowly heals back.

Each step the agent chooses one of three actions: go to the natural reward (H + 2), take the drug (H + 25, B − 1), or do nothing. It is never told which is best; it learns from the rewards it receives.

This is a computational hypothesis about dependence, built on homeostatic regulation and a shift towards drug use for relief. H and B are abstract variables, not measured brain quantities, and every number in the model is illustrative.

2. Meet the agent

The Environment panel shows the three stations. A card lights up when the agent chooses it, and the bottom line of each card shows how often it was chosen in the last 1,000 steps. Below the stations, the bar shows H (blue dot), B (gold mark), and the setpoint. The green band is the range natural rewards alone can hold H in.

Run it with the playback controls:

  • Step advances one decision: the agent chooses, its internal state changes, and it learns from the reward.
  • Play / Pause runs steps continuously. Speed changes only how fast the steps go on screen, not what the agent does.
  • Reset starts a brand-new agent that has learned nothing, keeping the current parameter settings.

A new agent knows nothing, so at first it often explores: 30% of its choices are random. That chance shrinks with every step, to 2% after about 3,000 steps, and from then on it mostly picks the action it has learned to value most.

Try it

  1. Press Step about ten times and watch which station the agent picks and what happens to H.
  2. Tick Show decision details. Step again and read the table: the action, whether it was random exploration or the highest learned value, and the reward it got.
  3. Press Play for a few seconds, then Pause, and read the percentages on the cards.
What should happen?

H stays close to 50. Doing nothing lets H sink towards B (40), and a natural reward lifts it again, so a trained agent ends up alternating: about half natural reward and half nothing. The rewards are tiny because H is already near the setpoint. Early on, some steps say random exploration; once the exploration chance is down to 2%, almost every step says highest learned value.

The Q-values shown with Show decision details are the agent’s learned estimates of total future reward for each action, not the reward of a single step. Section 4 explains the difference.

3. The tipping point

If the agent chose the natural reward every single step, H would settle at B + 20: each natural reward adds 2, and the pull back to B removes 10% of the gap. This is the natural-reward ceiling, the top of the green band.

At the healthy baseline of 40, the ceiling is 60, comfortably above the setpoint. But every drug use lowers B, and the ceiling falls with it. Once B drops below 30, the ceiling is below 50, and natural rewards can no longer bring H to the setpoint no matter how often the agent uses them. That baseline is the tipping point.

Try it

  1. Drag Baseline B from 40 down to 30, then to 10. How far short of the setpoint does H end up?
  2. Set B to 20, then raise Natural reward gain until the dot turns green.
What should happen?

At B = 30 natural rewards just reach 50. At B = 10 they only reach 30, 20 short. At B = 20 the dot turns green at a gain of 3: stronger natural rewards move the tipping point down to 20, giving the agent more room before natural rewards fail. That is one of the protective factors you can test in section 8.

Crossing the tipping point does not force the agent to take the drug. It only changes what natural rewards can achieve. Whether the agent turns to the drug depends on what it learns, which is the next question.

4. Why would the agent want the drug?

The reward for a step is how much closer it brings H to the setpoint. Moving away from 50 in either direction is punished, and the further H is from 50, the more each unit of movement is worth. Choosing the drug also adds a small pleasure bonus (2) and subtracts a harm cost (1).

So the same drug can be a bad or a good choice depending on the agent’s state. Explore the immediate reward of each action below.

Try it

  1. Press Healthy. Which action is worst, and why?
  2. Press Withdrawal. How does the ranking change?
  3. Starting from the healthy state, drag H down slowly. At roughly what level does the drug become the best single step?
What should happen?

In the healthy state the drug throws H from 50 to 74, far past the setpoint, and costs about −10.5: a content agent has no reason to use it. In withdrawal (H 20, B 0) the drug lifts H towards 50 and earns about +18, while the natural reward earns about 0 and doing nothing is punished. As you lower H with B at 40, the drug becomes the best single step once H is below about 37.

The agent uses Q-learning. For each combination of H and B, it keeps an estimate, a Q-value, of the total reward each action will bring now and over the following steps, and it adjusts those estimates after every step. A healthy agent rarely finds itself in withdrawal, so it has little chance to learn that the drug helps there. The next section makes it find out.

5. Making it happen: forced doses

This panel’s agent has already lived for 20,000 steps without the drug, so it has learned to keep H at the setpoint with natural rewards. Now the drug is available. The chart shows H (blue) and B (gold) over time, with dashed lines for the setpoint (50) and the tipping point (30).

Under Life events:

  • Drug available decides whether the agent can choose the drug.
  • Give 1 dose and Give 10 doses give the drug to the agent without it choosing: H jumps and B drops, but the agent gets no reward and learns nothing from the dose itself. It does then make its next choices from the changed state. Ten doses are given five steps apart.

On the chart, red marks are drug uses the agent chose and dark marks are forced doses.

Try it

  1. Press Play, then Give 10 doses once. Watch B, then let it run for a while.
  2. Press Restart panel, press Play, and this time click Give 10 doses twice in a row (20 doses).
  3. Set Show last to 5,000 steps to see the whole story, and compare where H and B end up.
What should happen?

10 doses: B falls to about 30, right at the tipping point, and then slowly heals. The agent goes back to natural rewards and does not choose the drug itself (in our test runs, 0 of 10 agents became dependent).

20 doses: B falls to about 21, well below the tipping point. Within a few dozen steps, red marks appear: the agent starts choosing the drug itself. Every use pushes B lower, so B sinks towards 0 and stays there (10 of 10 agents became dependent).

Look at H: the dependent agent keeps H near 50, not above it. It is not using the drug to get high but to get back to normal, because nothing else can. That is the feedback loop from section 1.

Two timescales are at work. H reacts within a few steps and then falls back towards B. B changes slowly, so the effect of a dose on H disappears long before its effect on B does. Without further use, B heals halfway back to 40 in about 700 steps.

Reading and navigating the chart
  • Show last picks how many steps are shown: 200, 500, 5,000 or 50,000. In wide windows the blue line is the average of H in each column, and the shading shows its range.
  • Y axis: − and + zoom, Fit keeps the axis fitted to the data shown, 0–100 shows the full range.
  • Time: − and + zoom in time, Now jumps back to the newest step and follows it.
  • Drag the chart to look back in time, and double-click it to reset the view. Explore the graph under the chart lists the scroll gestures.

6. What the agent has learned

The What the agent chooses map shows the agent’s current preference in every state. Baseline B runs left to right and H (labelled Current State (D)) bottom to top. Each square is coloured by the action with the highest Q-value there: grey for nothing, green for natural reward, red for drug. Pale squares have been visited too rarely to mean much. The dot is the agent now, and the fading line is its path over the last 100 steps. Hover over or tap a square for details.

Try it

  1. Before doing anything, look at which squares are coloured. Where has this agent been?
  2. Press Play, click Give 10 doses twice, and follow the dot.
  3. After a few thousand steps, hover over the red squares on the left.
What should happen?

At first only a few squares at the right, near B = 40 and H = 50, are coloured. That is the only place the healthy agent has been, so most of the map is still unknown to it. After the doses the dot moves left as B falls, and the agent experiences the low states for the first time. Red squares appear where H is low and B is low: there it has learned that the drug is worth the most. The agent now lives at the far left of the map, near B = 0.

This is why the forced doses matter: they take the agent to states it would almost never reach on its own, where it learns a new, drug-seeking policy. The dependence is in what it learned, not only in its current state.

7. Clean, at risk or dependent?

The Now panel summarises the agent at a glance: a status label with a one-line explanation, the step count, and the current H and B. Natural reward capacity tells you how high natural rewards alone could take H at the current baseline.

Try it

  1. Turn Speed right down to about 20 steps a second and press Play.
  2. Click Give 10 doses twice and watch the label and its explanation change.
What should happen?

The label starts as Clean. Partway through the doses, B drops below 30 and the label changes to At risk. A few hundred steps later, once the agent has chosen the drug on 5% of its last 1,000 steps, it changes to Dependent.

These labels describe the agent within this simulation. They are not clinical diagnoses.

8. Experiments: what makes dependence stick, and what protects against it

This panel combines everything: life events, the parameters, the Now panel and the chart. The five parameter sliders change the model while it runs, and the agent keeps what it has learned. Click the ⓘ next to a slider in the panel for a short explanation.

  • Drug harm cost (c): reward subtracted each time the agent chooses the drug. It does not change the drug’s effect on B.
  • Drug pleasure (R0): reward added each time the agent chooses the drug. It does not change how much H rises.
  • Baseline drop per drug use (δ): how far one dose lowers B.
  • Baseline healing rate (ρ): how quickly B recovers towards 40. At 0, it never recovers.
  • Natural reward gain: how much a natural reward raises H. Higher values lower the tipping point.

Experiment A: does cost stop a dependent agent?

  1. Press Play, click Give 10 doses twice, and wait until the label says Dependent.
  2. Raise Drug harm cost to 6 and let it run for a few thousand steps. Then try 10.
What should happen?

At a cost of 6 the agent keeps using the drug, even though a healthy agent facing the same cost never starts: relief from withdrawal outweighs the harm. At 10 the harm finally outweighs the relief, and the agent stops and slowly recovers. Continuing despite a cost that would deter a non-user is how the model captures compulsive use.

Experiment B: relapse

  1. Restart the panel. Make the agent dependent as before, and lower Speed so you can watch the step counter.
  2. Untick Drug available, wait about 500 steps, then tick it again.
  3. Restart and repeat, but wait about 2,000 steps before giving the drug back.
What should happen?

After 500 steps of abstinence, B has only healed to about 16, still far below the tipping point, and most agents go straight back to the drug (8 of 10 in our runs). After 2,000 steps, B is back around 35, natural rewards work again, and the agent stays clean. In this model, the only lasting trace of past use is the lowered baseline, so once B has healed, a former user behaves just like an agent that never used the drug.

Experiment C: protective factors

  1. Restart the panel. Before giving any doses, change one slider: Baseline healing rate to 0.005, or Natural reward gain to 4, or Baseline drop per drug use to 0.
  2. Press Play, give 20 doses as before, and compare with section 5.
What should happen?

Each change on its own prevents dependence. Faster healing pulls B back above the tipping point before the agent learns to use the drug. A stronger natural reward lowers the tipping point to 10, so natural rewards keep working. With no baseline drop, doses lift H briefly but never touch B, so the drug never becomes useful. This last one is the control: it shows that the baseline drop is the whole mechanism.

A caution about Reset: it gives a new agent that has learned nothing. If the drug is available from its very first step, its random exploration often leads it into the drug before it has learned to use natural rewards. To start again from a trained agent, use Restart panel, or untick Drug available, Reset, and run a few thousand steps at high speed before ticking it again.

9. What this model does and does not show

  • It shows that a single mechanism, a drug-induced drop in baseline, is enough to produce dependence, compulsive use despite rising costs, and relapse when the drug returns too soon.
  • All parameter values were chosen by hand to make the arithmetic easy to follow. They are not measured, and the exact numbers in this tutorial depend on them.
  • The model has no lasting relapse vulnerability: once B heals, nothing of past use remains. Real dependence leaves longer-lasting traces, such as drug-associated cues and changes in learning itself.
  • Outcomes vary from run to run because exploration is random. The figures quoted here come from ten test agents per experiment.
