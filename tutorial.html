How to use this tutorial

This is an interactive tutorial. You don’t just read about the simulation, you run it. Several sections contain a live panel: a framed piece of the real simulation with its own controls, such as Play and Step. Some figures have sliders you can drag as well.

  • One section at a time. Use the menu on the left to jump to any section, or the ← Previous and Next → buttons at the bottom of each section. The arrow keys work too.
  • Each panel has its own agent. What you do in one panel doesn’t affect the others, so experiment freely.
  • Want to start over? Press Restart panel, at the top right under a panel, to get that panel’s starting agent back. It undoes everything you did in that panel.
  • Try things out. Most sections end with a Try it box. Click it to open a short list of things to do in the panel above it.
  • Guess first, then check. Under each Try it box, click What should happen? to see the expected result. Make a guess before you open it: predicting first is the quickest way to find out whether the model works the way you think it does.

1. The main question

The main question explored in this simulation is: how can repeated drug use make a drug more and more valuable, even when every use carries a cost? It tests one possible answer: drug use gradually lowers an internal baseline, until natural rewards can no longer keep the agent where it wants to be, and the drug becomes the only thing that can.

The agent cares about two internal variables:

  • Internal level H: how the agent is doing right now. It wants H at a target called the setpoint, 50, and is rewarded for moving H towards it.
  • Baseline B: the level H drifts back to when nothing happens, like the agent’s resting mood. Each step, H moves 10% of the way towards B. B starts at 40. Each drug use lowers it by 1This is the default setting. You can change it with the Baseline drop per drug use (δ) slider in section 9; section 8 explains it., and without the drug it slowlyThis is the default setting. B gets halfway back in about 700 steps. You can change it with the Baseline healing rate (ρ) slider in section 9; section 8 explains it. heals back towards 40.

The baseline is loosely based on how the brain adapts to repeated drug use, known as allostasis (Koob & Le Moal, 2001).

Every step the agent takes, it chooses one of three actions. It is never told which is best; it learns from the rewards it receives.

  • Natural reward: raises H by 2This is the default setting. You can change it with the Natural reward gain slider in section 9; section 8 explains it..
  • Drug: raises H by 25, and lowers the baseline B by 1This is the default setting. You can change it with the Baseline drop per drug use (δ) slider in section 9; section 8 explains it..
  • Nothing: raises H by 0.

Whatever the agent chooses, H also drifts 10% of the way towards B in the same step. For example, with the default settings and starting from H = 50 and B = 40, the drift is −1, so after one step H is 49 after nothing, 51 after a natural reward, and 74 after the drug.

2. Meet the agent

In the Environment panel, the agent is the face that walks between its three options, each in its own box: natural reward, nothing and drug. When the agent chooses an option, that box lights up: green for natural reward, red for drug and grey for nothing. At the bottom of each box, a percentage shows how often the agent chose that option in its last 1,000 steps.

The face shows how the agent is doing. When H is at the setpoint it smiles and turns green . As H moves away from it, whether too low or too high, it turns yellow , then red, frowning with worried brows .

Below the options, the bar shows internal level H , baseline B , and the setpoint . The green band is the range natural rewards alone can hold H in.

Run it with these controls:

  • Step advances one decision: the agent chooses, its internal state changes, and it learns from the reward.
  • Play / Pause runs steps continuously.
  • Speed: drag the slider to change how fast the steps go on screen. It does not change what the agent does.
  • Reset starts a brand-new agent that has learned nothing, keeping the current parameter settings.
  • Show decision details shows what the agent has learned for each option, and why it made its last choice.

A new agent knows nothing, so at first it often exploresThe chance of a random choice is ε = max(0.02, 0.3 × 0.999t), where t is the number of steps the agent has lived.: 30% of its choices are random. That chance shrinks with every step, to 2% after about 2,700 steps, and from then on the agent mostly picks the action it has learned to value most.

Try it
  1. Press Step about ten times and watch which option the agent picks and what happens to H.
  2. Tick Show decision details. Step again and read the table: the action, whether it was random exploration or the highest learned value, and the reward it got.
  3. Press Play for a few seconds, then Pause, and read the percentages in the boxes.
What should happen?

H stays close to 50. Doing nothing lets H sink towards B (40), and a natural reward lifts it again, so a trained agent ends up alternating: about half natural reward and half nothing. The rewards are tiny because H is already near the setpoint. Early on, some steps say random exploration; once the exploration chance is down to 2%, almost every step says highest learned value.

These numbers are for the default settings. They change if you move the Natural reward gain slider (section 8 explains them).

With Show decision details ticked, each box shows the agent’s learned estimate of how much reward that option will bring in total, now and over the following steps. This estimate is called a Q-value. It is not the reward of a single step; section 4 explains the difference.

Try it
  1. Drag Baseline B from 40 down to 30, then to 10. How far short of the setpoint does H end up?
  2. Set B to 20, then raise Natural reward gain until the dot turns green.
What should happen?

At B = 30 natural rewards just reach 50. At B = 10 they only reach 30, 20 short. At B = 20 the dot turns green at a gain of 3: stronger natural rewards move the tipping point down to 20, giving the agent more room before natural rewards fail. That is one of the protective factors you can test in section 9.

Crossing the tipping point does not force the agent to take the drug. It only changes what natural rewards can achieve. Whether the agent turns to the drug depends on what it learns, which is the next question.

4. Why would the agent want the drug?

The reward for a step is how much closer it brings H to the setpoint. Moving away from 50 in either direction is punished, and the further H is from 50, the more each unit of movement is worth.

Choosing the drug also adds a pleasure bonus (+2)The drug feels good in itself, the “high”. The agent gets this extra reward every time it takes the drug, on top of what the drug does to H:drug reward = reward from H + 2 (pleasure) − 1 (harm)The default value. You can change it with the Drug pleasure slider in section 9. and subtracts a harm cost (−1)The damage that comes with each use, such as to health, money or relationships. It is subtracted from the reward every time the agent takes the drug:drug reward = reward from H + 2 (pleasure) − 1 (harm)The default value. You can change it with the Drug harm cost slider in section 9., whatever happens to H.

So the same drug can be a bad or a good choice depending on the agent’s state. Below, the three options are shown as in the Environment panel. Set H and B, and each box shows where that option would take H and the reward the agent would get for it, for one step. The best choice is outlined, and the agent stands under it. Its face shows how it feels at the current H.

Try it
  1. Press Healthy (H = 50, B = 40). Which action is worst, and why?
  2. Press In withdrawal (H = 20, B = 0). How does the ranking change?
  3. Starting from the healthy state, drag H down slowly. At roughly what level does the drug become the best single step?
What should happen?

In the healthy state the drug throws H from 50 to 74, far past the setpoint, and costs about −10.5: a content agent has no reason to use it. In withdrawal (H 20, B 0) the drug lifts H towards 50 and earns about +18, while the natural reward earns about 0 and doing nothing is punished. As you lower H with B at 40, the drug becomes the best single step once H is below about 37.

These numbers are for the default settings. They change if you move the Drug harm cost, Drug pleasure and Natural reward gain sliders (section 8 explains them).

The agent learns which option is best by Q-learningFor each combination of H and B, the agent keeps a Q-value for every option: its estimate of the total reward that option will bring, now and over the following steps. After every step it compares the reward it actually got with what it expected, and nudges that Q-value a little towards what happened. Over many steps the estimates get better, and the agent picks the option with the highest one., a way of learning by trial and error. A healthy agent rarely finds itself in withdrawal, so it has little chance to learn that the drug helps there. The next section makes it find out.

5. Forced doses: pushing the agent past the tipping point

In this section we explore what happens when a healthy agent is given the drug without choosing it: do a few forced doses push B below the tipping point, and does the agent then start choosing the drug itself?

This panel’s agent has already lived for 20,000 steps without the drug, so it has learned to keep H at the setpoint with natural rewards. Now the drug is available.

The agent here is the same as in the earlier panels: the same H, the same B and the same three options. Only the way it is shown is different. Instead of the option boxes and the H bar, this panel draws H and B as lines over time, so you can see how they change step by step. It shows H and B over time, with dashed lines for the setpoint (50) and the tipping point (30This number is for the default settings. It changes if you move the Natural reward gain slider (section 8 explains them).).

Under Life events:

  • Drug available decides whether the agent can choose the drug.
  • Give 1 dose and Give 10 doses give the drug to the agent without it choosing: H jumps and B drops, but the agent gets no reward and learns nothing from the dose itself. It does then make its next choices from the changed state. Ten doses are given five steps apart.

On the chart, red marks along the bottom are drug uses the agent chose, and dark marks are forced doses.

Try it
  1. Press Play, then Give 10 doses once. Watch B, then let it run for a while.
  2. Press Restart panel, press Play, and this time click Give 10 doses twice in a row (20 doses).
  3. Set Show last to 5,000 steps to see the whole story, and compare where H and B end up.
What should happen?

10 doses: B falls to about 30, right at the tipping point, and then slowly heals. The agent goes back to natural rewards and does not choose the drug itself (in our test runs, 0 of 10 agents became dependent).

20 doses: B falls to about 21, well below the tipping point. Within a few dozen steps, red marks appear: the agent starts choosing the drug itself. Every use pushes B lower, so B sinks towards 0 and stays there (10 of 10 agents became dependent).

Look at H: the dependent agent keeps H near 50, not above it. It is not using the drug to get high but to get back to normal, because nothing else can. That is the feedback loop from section 1.

These numbers are for the default settings. They change if you move the Drug harm cost, Drug pleasure, Baseline drop per drug use, Baseline healing rate and Natural reward gain sliders (section 8 explains them).

Two timescales are at work. H reacts within a few steps and then falls back towards B. B changes slowly, so the effect of a dose on H disappears long before its effect on B does. Try it
  1. Before doing anything, look at which squares are coloured. Where has this agent been?
  2. Press Play, click Give 10 doses twice, and follow the dot.
  3. After a few thousand steps, hover over the red squares on the left.
What should happen?

At first only a few squares at the right, near B = 40 and H = 50, are coloured. That is the only place the healthy agent has been, so most of the map is still unknown to it. After the doses the dot moves left as B falls, and the agent experiences the low states for the first time. Red squares appear where H is low and B is low: there it has learned that the drug is worth the most. The agent now lives at the far left of the map, near B = 0.

This is why the forced doses matter: they take the agent to states it would almost never reach on its own, where it learns a new, drug-seeking policy. The dependence is in what it learned, not only in its current state.

The Now panel summarises the agent at a glance: a status label, Clean, At risk or Dependent, with a one-line explanation, the step count, and the current H and B. Natural reward capacity tells you how high natural rewards alone could take H at the current baseline.

The label starts as Clean. Partway through the doses, B drops below 30 and the label changes to At risk. A few hundred steps later, once the agent has chosen the drug on 5% of its last 1,000 steps, it changes to Dependent.

8. The parameters

So far every number in the model has been fixed. Five of them are parameters: settings you can change with the sliders in the next section, to test what makes dependence more or less likely. Each one is listed below with its default value and what it means. Hover over a name to see the formula it appears in.

  • 1. Drug harm cost (c)It is subtracted from the reward each time the agent chooses the drug:drug reward = reward from H + R0 − c

    A penalty the agent gets every time it takes the drug. It stands for the damage drug use does, for example to health, money or relationships.

    default (1)
  • 2. Drug pleasure (R0)It is added to the reward each time the agent chooses the drug:drug reward = reward from H + R0 − c

    A bonus the agent gets every time it takes the drug. It stands for the “high”: the drug feels good, apart from what it does to H.

    default (2)
  • 3. Baseline drop per drug use (δ)Each step, B changes by:new B = B − δ (if the drug was taken) + ρ × (40 − B)

    How much one dose lowers the baseline B. It stands for how strongly the brain adapts to the drug.

    default (1)
  • 4. Baseline healing rate (ρ)Each step, B changes by:new B = B − δ (if the drug was taken) + ρ × (40 − B)

    How fast B recovers towards 40 when the agent stops using the drug. At the default, B gets halfway back in about 700 steps.

    default (0.001)
  • 5. Natural reward gainAfter a natural reward, H changes by:new H = H + gain + 0.1 × (B − H)

    How much H goes up each time the agent takes a natural reward.

    default (2)

The other numbers in the model stay fixed:

  • Setpoint: 50, the level of H the agent wants.
  • Healthy baseline: 40, where B starts and heals back to.
  • Natural reward: raises H by 2 (unless you change the natural reward gain).
  • Drug: raises H by 25.
  • Drift: each step, H moves 10% of the way towards B.

The first two parameters change how rewarding the drug feels; the last three change what drug use does to the agent’s body over time. Section 9 shows why this difference matters. Once an agent is dependent, making the drug more costly barely helps: the agent keeps using it until the cost is made very high, because the relief from withdrawal outweighs the harm. The baseline parameters work earlier: they decide whether the agent becomes dependent in the first place.

9. Experiments: what makes dependence stick, and what protects against it

This panel combines everything: life events, the parameter sliders from section 8, the Now panel and the chart. The sliders change the model while it runs, and the agent keeps what it has learned. Click the ⓘ next to a slider in the panel for a short explanation.

Experiment A: does cost stop a dependent agent?
  1. Press Play, click Give 10 doses twice, and wait until the label says Dependent.
  2. Raise Drug harm cost to 6 and let it run for a few thousand steps. Then try 10.
What should happen?

At a cost of 6 the agent keeps using the drug, even though a healthy agent facing the same cost never starts: relief from withdrawal outweighs the harm. At 10 the harm finally outweighs the relief, and the agent stops and slowly recovers. Continuing despite a cost that would deter a non-user is how the model captures compulsive use.

Experiment B: relapse
  1. Restart the panel. Make the agent dependent as before, and lower Speed so you can watch the step counter.
  2. Untick Drug available, wait about 500 steps, then tick it again.
  3. Restart and repeat, but wait about 2,000 steps before giving the drug back.
What should happen?

After 500 steps of abstinence, B has only healed to about 16, still far below the tipping point, and most agents go straight back to the drug (8 of 10 in our runs). After 2,000 steps, B is back around 35, natural rewards work again, and the agent stays clean. In this model, the only lasting trace of past use is the lowered baseline, so once B has healed, a former user behaves just like an agent that never used the drug.

These numbers are for the default settings. They change if you move the Drug harm cost, Drug pleasure, Baseline drop per drug use, Baseline healing rate and Natural reward gain sliders (section 8 explains them).

Experiment C: protective factors
  1. Restart the panel. Before giving any doses, change one slider: Baseline healing rate to 0.005, or Natural reward gain to 4, or Baseline drop per drug use to 0.
  2. Press Play, give 20 doses as before, and compare with section 5.
What should happen?

Each change on its own prevents dependence. Faster healing pulls B back above the tipping point before the agent learns to use the drug. A stronger natural reward lowers the tipping point to 10, so natural rewards keep working. With no baseline drop, doses lift H briefly but never touch B, so the drug never becomes useful. This last one is the control: it shows that the baseline drop is the whole mechanism.

A caution about Reset: it gives a new agent that has learned nothing. If the drug is available from its very first step, its random exploration often leads it into the drug before it has learned to use natural rewards. To start again from a trained agent, use Restart panel, or untick Drug available, Reset, and run a few thousand steps at high speed before ticking it again.

10. What this model does and does not show

  • It shows that a single mechanism, a drug-induced drop in baseline, is enough to produce dependence, compulsive use despite rising costs, and relapse when the drug returns too soon.
  • All parameter values were chosen by hand to make the arithmetic easy to follow. They are not measured, and the exact numbers in this tutorial depend on them.
  • The model has no lasting relapse vulnerability: once B heals, nothing of past use remains. Real dependence leaves longer-lasting traces, such as drug-associated cues and changes in learning itself.
  • Outcomes vary from run to run because exploration is random. The figures quoted here come from ten test agents per experiment.
